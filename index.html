The problem cm/360 doesn't solve

cm/360 is a great number, but it only describes what happens inside the game engine. Move your mouse 1 cm and the camera rotates by a fixed number of in-game degrees. That much really is constant across setups.

What it cannot tell you is how far the crosshair appears to move across your vision — and that is what your aim is actually calibrated to. Two things break the link, and cm/360 is blind to both.

Blind spot 1 — screen geometry. Both panels sit at the same 60 cm; only the width differs. The 27-inch span nests inside the 32-inch span, so the wider screen captures a larger cone of your vision and the same mouse movement sweeps more degrees across your retina.

cm/360 is also blind to FOV

This one catches people out more often, because it happens without changing a single piece of hardware. Two players on identical monitors, identical seats, identical cm/360 — but different FOV sliders — do not have the same visual sensitivity. Not even close.

The reason is that FOV decides how much of the game world gets squeezed onto the same piece of glass. Raise your FOV and every object shrinks, including the distance your crosshair travels for a given rotation. The engine still turns exactly the same number of degrees per centimetre. But those degrees now buy you less screen.

Blind spot 2 — field of view. At FOV 80 one in-game degree moves the crosshair 0.62 cm across a 27-inch screen. At FOV 120 it moves only 0.30 cm. Identical mouse movement, half the visual result.

The chain, one link at a time

VAS fixes both blind spots by following the movement all the way from your hand to your retina. There are three links, and each one has real physical units.

Prerequisite — monitors are sold by diagonal, but every step below needs horizontal width. For 16:9 the conversion factor is 0.8716.

Link 1 — your hand becomes rotation. This is the part cm/360 already handles. Divide 360 by your cm/360 and you have in-game degrees per centimetre of mouse movement.

360 / 30 = 12.0 in-game degrees per cm

Link 2 — rotation becomes distance across the glass. This is where FOV enters. The game projects the world onto your screen through a tangent relationship, and at the crosshair the scale works out to:

projection scale = (W / 2) / tan( FOV / 2 )  →  29.89 / 1.257 = 23.77 cm per radian = 0.415 cm per degree

The tan(FOV/2) is not a fudge factor. It is the actual geometry of how a rectilinear projection maps angles onto a flat plane — the same math the renderer itself uses to build every frame.

Link 3 — distance becomes visual angle. Your eye sits at distance d. A small movement across the glass subtends an angle, and at the centre of the screen that works out to simply 1/d:

eye scale = 1 / d  →  1 / 60 radians per cm = 0.955 visual degrees per cm

The full chain at the default setup: 27 inch, 60 cm, FOV 103°, 30 cm/360. Multiply the three link factors together and you get VAS directly.

Collapsing links 2 and 3 into a single number gives K, the combined gain:

K = (W / 2) / ( d · tan( FOV / 2 ) ) = 0.396
VAS = (360 / cm360) × K = 12.0 × 0.396 = 4.76 deg/cm

What K actually means

K has an interpretation that makes the whole metric click. Rearranged, it is a ratio of two distances:

K = dcorrect / dactual   where  dcorrect = (W / 2) / tan( FOV / 2 )

Every FOV setting implies one viewing distance at which the image is geometrically honest — where your eye sits exactly where the virtual camera does and the monitor becomes a literal window. The engine neither knows nor enforces this; it just falls out of the projection math.

For a 27-inch screen at FOV 103°, that distance is 23.8 cm. Nobody sits that close, which is why K lands near 0.4 for most competitive players. Running wide FOV at a normal desk distance means deliberately accepting a fisheye view — a rational trade, since peripheral information wins more fights than correct perspective does.

When K = 1 you are sitting at the correct distance, and one in-game degree equals exactly one visual degree.

Where the tangent belongs — and where it doesn't

A tangent shows up once in this chain, in link 2, converting FOV into a projection scale. It belongs there because that is the projection geometry.

A common mistake is to add a second tangent wrapped around the mouse-movement term, usually justified as "correcting for rectilinear distortion." That one is wrong, and it is wrong for a specific reason: your cm/360 was measured by physically turning a full circle, so it already captures how the engine maps mouse counts to rotation. Applying a tangent to it double-counts a correction that is already baked in.

The test is simple. Does the term describe how angles land on glass? If yes, tangent. If it describes how your mouse produces rotation, cm/360 already covered it.

Reading the number

  • Higher VAS — more of your vision sweeps per cm. Feels faster, favours flicking.
  • Lower VAS — less visual movement per cm. Feels slower, favours tracking and precision.
  • Equal VAS — two setups feel the same to your eye, regardless of monitor, seat, game, or FOV.
  • K near 1 — your FOV and seating are geometrically matched. Rare in competitive play.

VAS is quoted at the crosshair, which is where aiming happens. Because K ≠ 1 for most setups, the gain is not perfectly uniform across the screen — the periphery runs roughly twice as fast at typical competitive FOV. That is a property of choosing wide FOV, not something a sensitivity formula can fix.

What VAS does not account for

  • Monitor curvature — the math assumes a flat panel. Curved screens hold their edges closer to your eye than the formula predicts.
  • Off-centre viewing — it assumes your eye sits centred and perpendicular to the screen.
  • FOV conventions — games specify FOV as horizontal, vertical, or Hor+. Feed it a vertical number and the answer will be confidently wrong.
  • ADS and scope multipliers — separate per-game scalars. VAS describes hipfire only.
  • Mouse acceleration — the whole model assumes a linear counts-to-rotation mapping.

VAS (Visual Angular Sensitivity) measures degrees of your visual field per centimetre of mouse movement, evaluated at the crosshair. Unlike raw cm/360, it accounts for monitor size, viewing distance and in-game FOV — so two setups with the same VAS feel identical to your eye.
